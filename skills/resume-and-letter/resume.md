# Tailoring a resume

The goal: the same person, the same facts, arranged so this employer sees the fit fast. A person should get a job on what they have done, not on how well they write resumes. The truth rules in `SKILL.md` come first; nothing here overrides them.

## 1. Read the posting

Pull five things out of it and show them to the person in a short list:

1. **Must-haves:** the requirements stated as required.
2. **Nice-to-haves:** the ones stated as preferred or a plus.
3. **Key words:** the tools, skills and terms the posting repeats.
4. **Between the lines:** what the posting signals without saying it (pace, team size, customer-facing or not).
5. **Kind of role:** hands-on doer, people leader, or a coordinator across teams.

Ask: "Is this how you read it? Anything you know about the job that is not in the posting?" What the person knows beats what the posting says. Work from the posting and the person's answer only; do not research the company or look up people who hold the job.

## 2. Read the resume

List every role with its title, employer and dates exactly as written, then the bullets under each, the skills, and the education. Note the length, the section order and how bullets are written (verb first, numbers or not). The tailored version keeps that style unless the person asks for a change.

## 3. Plan the shape

Decide, and show the person before you rewrite anything:

- **Section order.** Experience first for most people; education first for a recent graduate or where the posting requires a degree.
- **Which roles get the most bullets.** More for the roles closest to the posting, fewer for the rest. Older roles that do not fit can shrink to one line.
- **Several roles at one employer.** Keep them separate when each one matters to this posting; group them under the employer with each title and its dates kept exact when space is tight. Different employers are never merged.
- **Titles stay as written.** If a title hides what the work was, keep the title and let the first bullet say what the work was. Never swap in a title the person did not hold.
- **The summary.** Two or three lines that name the person's strongest match to the must-haves, using only facts already on the page.

## 4. Match each bullet to the posting

For each must-have, find the best line the person already has and sort it into one of four bands:

| Band | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | Same skill, same kind of work | Use it, lead with it |
| Transferable | Same skill, different setting | Use it, word it so the skill shows |
| Adjacent | Related tool, method or problem | Use it if nothing better; word it honestly |
| Gap | Nothing on the resume fits | Say so; see step 5 |

Weigh a line mostly on whether it is a direct match, then on whether the skill carries over, then on how close it sits, and last on whether the result it shows (money saved, people led, speed) is the kind this posting values. You do not need a number for this; the band is enough.

## 5. Ask about the gaps

For each gap or weak match, ask once, in plain words, and follow the answer:

- **A skill gap:** "The posting asks for [skill]. Have you used it, or something close to it?"
  - Yes: ask what for, how big, and whether there was a result they can name.
  - Indirectly: ask what their part was. If it was real support work, it can be worded as support.
  - Something close: ask about that instead. Mention it only if it is honestly close.
  - On their own time (a course, a side project, volunteering): ask how recent and what they made. Include it if it is real and recent.
  - No: try one broader question, then move on.
- **A soft skill** (leading, persuading, handling customers): "Tell me about a time you [did that]." If the answer is vague, ask it a different way once, with an example.
- **Recent work:** "What have you done in the last six months that is not on your resume yet?" Small things count: a new tool, training someone, fixing a process.

Stop after two or three tries on one gap. Some gaps stay gaps. Write down each answer as the person said it, draft it as a bullet, and ask: "Is this accurate?" Only a confirmed answer goes on the resume.

## 6. Reword, never re-fact

Four honest ways to make a true line fit better:

- **Use the posting's word for the same thing.** "Ran the stock counts" becomes "managed inventory accuracy" if that is what the counts were for.
- **Lead with the part this employer cares about.** Same facts, the result first: "Cut returns 12% by redesigning the packing check" instead of "Redesigned the packing check, which cut returns 12%."
- **Adjust the detail.** Name the tool when the posting names tools; drop it when the posting cares about the outcome.
- **Show the scope that is there.** "Coordinated with the warehouse, sales and billing teams" if three teams were involved, not "worked with others".

Each change keeps every fact: the number, the date, the title, who did what. If a reword needs a fact that is not in the original or in an answer, it is not a reword. Cut it.

## 7. Hand it over

Give the person:

1. **The tailored resume in Markdown,** in the person's own section style.
2. **The list of changes,** one line each: reworded (original and new), moved, cut, or added, and for each addition the answer it came from.
3. **The gaps left,** each with one honest option (leave out, cover in the cover letter, or more to find).
4. **The offer:** "Want this as a Word file or a PDF?" Then `document-builder` on a yes.

If the content runs past the original length, list the weakest lines by band and ask which to cut. Keep the original resume untouched; the tailored one is a new file.
